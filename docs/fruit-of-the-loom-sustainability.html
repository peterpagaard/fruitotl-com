Is Fruit of the Loom sustainable? An honest look at certifications, cotton and the Morocco factory

Updated: 2026-09-11

Sustainability is now a standard question for anyone ordering club hoodies, company tees or just a pack of basics. Fruit of the Loom is cheap and everywhere — but is it a responsible choice? We checked the certifications, the factory set-up and the parent company's own figures, and compared the result with organic alternatives.

Is Fruit of the Loom a sustainable brand? The short version

Fruit of the Loom is not an eco-label. It is a mass-market basics brand owned by Berkshire Hathaway, it produces mostly conventional cotton, and it competes on price. If your first criterion is organic fibre and third-party social certification of the whole chain, brands such as Stanley/Stella are built for that — at roughly two to three times the price.

That said, the picture is better than the "cheap basics" reputation suggests. The European range is produced at a vertically integrated factory in Morocco, the products carry the OEKO-TEX Standard 100 label, the company has been an amfori BSCI member for more than 15 years and takes part in the Better Cotton programme. And because the garments are durable, a t-shirt that survives five years of washing has a lower footprint per wear than a fashion tee that is thrown out after one season.

Our verdict in one line: a reasonable, honestly documented mid-field choice — clearly better than anonymous no-name basics, clearly behind the organic specialists.

Where and how is the European range made?

This is the strongest part of the story. Instead of buying finished garments from a chain of sub-contractors, Fruit of the Loom International runs its own factory in Morocco, where yarn, knitting, dyeing, cutting, sewing and packing happen under one roof. According to the retailer Marmalade Co., which collects its stock directly at that factory, the site employs more than 1,000 people, uses automated cutting to optimise fabric use and minimise offcuts, and runs a dye house designed for minimal water use with recycling of dye-bath water.

Three things follow from a single owned factory rather than outsourced production:

  • Traceability. One site, one workforce, one set of audits — far easier to check than a dozen anonymous suppliers.
  • Short transport. Morocco is a short sea crossing from Spain. A garment sewn there travels a fraction of the distance of one made in South or East Asia.
  • Short supply chain. A retailer that buys at the factory gate has a three-step chain (factory → retailer → you) instead of five. That is mostly a price argument, but fewer warehouses and re-shipments also mean fewer transport emissions.

What does the parent company report on climate and water?

The corporate group publishes a sustainability roadmap called Fruitful Futures. The figures below are the group's own, cover the global business (largely the US operation) and are not independently audited on the page we checked — so treat them as company claims rather than verified facts:

  • 73 % renewable electricity in 2024, with a stated goal of 100 % by 2030.
  • 23 % lower water intensity since 2018 (water per unit produced).
  • 92 % of cotton from sustainable suppliers in the US in 2024, and a target of 100 % of the supply chain mapped back to raw materials.
  • The full supply chain is disclosed online, and a Forced Labor Report is published on the European site.

How much of that applies to the tee you buy in Dublin or Berlin is hard to say, because the European range is made in Morocco from cotton sourced through the Better Cotton programme, not from US farms. The renewable-electricity and water figures are group-wide averages. We would like to see a Europe-specific breakdown; until then, the honest position is "encouraging direction, incomplete data".

Durability: the sustainability factor nobody counts

Life-cycle studies of cotton garments consistently show that the growing and dyeing phases dominate the footprint, and that the single biggest lever a consumer has is how many times the garment is worn. A 280 g/m² Classic Hooded Sweat (80 % cotton / 20 % polyester) that is still in service after five winters has been "diluted" over hundreds of wears; an organic hoodie discarded after one season has not.

Fruit of the Loom scores well here. The heavier tees (Valueweight T at approx. 165 g/m², Heavy Cotton T at approx. 195 g/m²) and the 280 g/m² sweats are known for keeping shape and colour through many washes. To get the most wears out of them:

  • Wash at 30–40 °C; 100 % cotton can shrink a few percent on a hot first wash.
  • Avoid hot tumble drying — it is the main cause of shrinkage and pilling.
  • Turn printed garments inside out to protect the print.
  • Buy the right size the first time using our size guide; a tee that fits gets worn, a tee that doesn't gets binned.

Our conclusion

Fruit of the Loom is neither green nor a black sheep. It has a real owned factory with water-saving dyeing, product-level chemical safety via OEKO-TEX, long-standing social audits via amfori BSCI and a cotton programme that is better than the baseline — but it is not organic, and its climate data is group-level and self-reported. Buy fewer, buy the right size, wash cool, and wear it for years: that is where most of the sustainability lives.

Frequently asked questions

Is Fruit of the Loom OEKO-TEX certified?

Yes. The European range carries the OEKO-TEX Standard 100 label, which means the finished textile has been tested for more than 100 potentially harmful substances and is safe for skin contact. Retailers such as Marmalade Co. show the certificate on product pages. Note that OEKO-TEX covers chemical safety only — it says nothing about water use, emissions or working conditions.

Does Fruit of the Loom use organic cotton?

Mostly no. The core European range (Valueweight T, Original T, Classic Hooded Sweat and so on) is made from conventional cotton sourced through the Better Cotton programme, which reduces water and pesticide use at farm level but is not organic. The European catalogue has a small organic sub-range; if you need GOTS-certified garments, brands like Stanley/Stella are the safer choice.

Where are Fruit of the Loom clothes made for Europe?

At the group's own vertically integrated factory in Morocco. Knitting, dyeing, cutting, sewing and packing happen under one roof, with more than 1,000 employees, automated cutting to reduce waste and a dye house that recycles dye-bath water. Morocco is close to Spain, so transport to European customers is short compared with Asian production.

Are Fruit of the Loom working conditions audited?

The company has been a member of amfori BSCI, one of the largest social-compliance audit systems, for more than 15 years, and publishes a Forced Labor Report on its European site. Audits cover working hours, pay, health and safety at production sites. Individual audit reports are not public, so you have to rely on the membership and the published code of conduct.

Is Fruit of the Loom more sustainable than Gildan or fast fashion?

Roughly on par with Gildan — both are conventional-cotton basics brands with OEKO-TEX and their own ESG reporting. Compared with fast-fashion tees, the advantage is durability: a 165 g/m² Valueweight T or a 280 g/m² Classic sweat is built to survive years of washing, which lowers the footprint per wear. Organic specialists remain clearly ahead on fibre and social certification.

What is the most sustainable way to buy Fruit of the Loom?

Buy the right size using the size chart, order once rather than in several small parcels, and wash at 30–40 °C without hot tumble drying so the garment lasts.
