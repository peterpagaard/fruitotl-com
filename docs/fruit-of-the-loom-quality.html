Is Fruit of the Loom good quality? An honest assessment

Updated: 2026-09-11

Fruit of the Loom sells itself as dependable basics at a low price — but does a €6.95 T-shirt or a €20.95 hoodie actually hold up? We looked at the fabrics, weights, construction and care behaviour of the European range, and compared it with the brands it competes against. The short version: it is very good for its price bracket, and honest about what it is not.

Quick answer

Yes — Fruit of the Loom is solid quality for its price segment. Tees use ring-spun, combed cotton at approx. 145–195 g/m²; sweatshirts and hoodies are 280 g/m² in 80 % cotton / 20 % polyester, which limits shrinkage. Washed at 30–40 °C without hot tumble drying, the garments keep shape and colour through years of regular washing. It is not premium like Stanley/Stella (2–3× the price), but at €6.95 per T-shirt and €20.95 per hoodie at Marmalade Co. it outperforms most basics in its class.

What is Fruit of the Loom actually made of?

Quality in basics starts with the yarn. Fruit of the Loom uses ring-spun cotton across its classic range and combed cotton in the premium lines. Ring spinning twists the fibres tightly into a fine, strong yarn; combing removes the short fibres first, leaving a smoother, more even thread. The result is a fabric that feels softer than open-end (carded) cotton, pills less and holds print more evenly.

Solid-colour tees are 100 % cotton. Heather or melange colours contain a share of polyester — that is what creates the marbled look, and it is normal across the industry. Many of the sweats are knitted with Belcoro® yarn, a Fruit of the Loom yarn developed to give a smoother surface for printing.

Sweatshirts and hoodies are a different recipe: the Classic Hooded Sweat and Classic Set-In Sweat are 280 g/m² in 80 % cotton / 20 % polyester, the Premium Hooded is 70/30. The polyester share is a deliberate quality choice, not a cost cut — it reduces shrinkage, speeds drying and keeps the ribbing elastic after many washes.

How durable is Fruit of the Loom in real use?

Durability is where the brand earns its reputation. The classic tees are knitted as a tube — no side seams — which removes the two seams most likely to twist or split. Necks are ribbed and hold their shape. None of this is exotic; it is simply done consistently, batch after batch, which is what you want from a basic.

In everyday terms, a Valueweight T washed weekly at 30–40 °C keeps its shape and colour for years, with the collar staying flat and the hem straight. The Heavy Cotton T lasts longer still because there is simply more fabric to wear through. Hoodies show wear first at the cuffs and the pocket opening, and later as slight pilling on the inside of the sleeves — typical for any 80/20 sweat, and cosmetic rather than structural.

Where you will notice the price: the tees do not have the dense, silky hand-feel of a €30 fashion tee, white Original Ts are a little sheer, and colours fade very slowly over a couple of years of sun and washing. For a garment that costs €6.95, none of that is a fair complaint.

Does Fruit of the Loom shrink?

The most common quality complaint about any cotton basic is shrinkage, so it deserves a straight answer. A 100 % cotton Fruit of the Loom tee can shrink a few per cent — mostly in length — if the first wash is hot or the shirt goes through a hot tumble cycle. Washed at 30–40 °C and air dried, the change is barely measurable. The 80/20 sweats shrink noticeably less because the polyester does not move.

  • Wash at 30 °C for colour, 40 °C at most.
  • Tumble dry on low or not at all; hang to dry in the shade.
  • Turn printed garments inside out.
  • If you are between two sizes, take the larger one — our Fruit of the Loom size guide has the charts in cm.

Where is Fruit of the Loom made, and does it matter?

Fruit of the Loom is a US brand (founded 1851, owned by Berkshire Hathaway since 2002), with its European business run from Kaiserslautern, Germany. Fruit of the Loom also runs its own factory in Morocco — the source of the garments sold by Marmalade Co. — a vertically integrated site where the yarn is spun, knitted, dyed, cut and sewn under one roof by more than 1,000 employees, with automated cutting and water-saving dyeing.

That matters for quality in two ways. First, one factory controlling the whole process means consistent fabric weight, colour and sizing from batch to batch — the reason a navy Valueweight T bought this year matches one bought three years ago. Second, it keeps shipping distances to Europe short. Marmalade Co., the cheapest verified retailer in our comparisons, buys directly from that factory and collects there, cutting the supply chain from five steps to three; that is how it reaches €6.95 per T-shirt rather than the €9–14 typical elsewhere. The shop also shows an OEKO-TEX certificate on product pages, so the fabrics are tested for harmful substances.

How does Fruit of the Loom compare with the competition?

Fruit of the Loom vs Gildan

The two giants of the basics market are closer than their fans admit. Gildan's Heavy Cotton 5000 uses open-end cotton at approx. 180 g/m² with a boxier US-style cut; Fruit of the Loom's ring-spun Valueweight is softer with a trimmer European fit. Gildan is often marginally cheaper at wholesale, Fruit of the Loom is easier to find in Europe. Full comparison in our Fruit of the Loom vs Gildan article.

Fruit of the Loom vs B&C and Sol's

B&C and Sol's compete in the same price bracket with comparable ring-spun fabrics and broad colour cards. Differences are mostly in fit and availability; Fruit of the Loom has the wider retail presence and the lowest verified consumer prices.

Fruit of the Loom vs Stanley/Stella

Stanley/Stella is the premium alternative: organic cotton, fashion-led fits and full sustainability certification, at roughly two to three times the price. The fabric is noticeably nicer in the hand. Whether it is worth it depends on the job — for a brand's retail merchandise, perhaps; for a team, an event or a wardrobe of everyday basics, Fruit of the Loom delivers far more per euro.

Fruit of the Loom vs H&M and Uniqlo basics

High-street basics at €8–15 are usually lighter (around 140–150 g/m²), use carded or blended cotton and are cut for a season's fashion rather than years of washing. Fruit of the Loom is plainer but heavier and more consistent, and the quantity discounts have no high-street equivalent.

Our verdict: who should buy Fruit of the Loom?

Fruit of the Loom is exactly what it claims to be: reliable, consistent cotton basics that survive years of washing and cost a fraction of fashion-brand prices. It is not premium and does not pretend to be. It is the right choice for:

  • Everyday wardrobes — a stack of tees and a couple of hoodies that just work.
  • Printing — school, club, event and company garments where the surface and the price per unit matter.
  • Families — kids' clothes that get washed three times a week and outgrown before they wear out.

It is the wrong choice if you want a luxurious hand-feel or fashion-forward cuts — look at Stanley/Stella or a retail brand for that. For everyone else, the value is hard to beat, especially at Marmalade Co.'s verified €6.95 for a tee and €20.95 for a hoodie, with 10 % off from five pieces.

Frequently asked questions

Is Fruit of the Loom good quality?

Yes, for its price bracket. The tees use ring-spun or combed cotton at approx. 145–195 g/m², the sweats are 280 g/m² in 80/20 cotton-polyester, and construction details such as the tubular body are consistent batch to batch. It is not a premium brand, but it outlasts most basics that cost the same or more.

Which cotton does Fruit of the Loom use?

Ring-spun cotton in the classic range and combed cotton in the premium lines; both are smoother and stronger than open-end cotton. Solid colours are 100 % cotton, heather colours contain some polyester, and many sweats are knitted with Belcoro® yarn for a smoother print surface. Sweatshirts and hoodies are 80 % cotton / 20 % polyester.

Does Fruit of the Loom shrink in the wash?

A 100 % cotton tee can shrink a few per cent, mainly in length, if washed hot or tumble dried on high the first time. At 30–40 °C with air drying the change is minimal. The 80/20 sweats are more stable. If you are between sizes, choose the larger one to be safe.

How long does Fruit of the Loom clothing last?

Washed weekly at 30–40 °C and air dried, a Valueweight T keeps its shape and colour for years; the Heavy Cotton T lasts longer still. Hoodies show cosmetic wear at the cuffs and slight pilling inside the sleeves before anything structural fails. Hot washing and hot tumble drying are what shorten the lifespan.

Is Fruit of the Loom better than Gildan or Stanley/Stella?

Against Gildan it is a near tie: Fruit of the Loom is softer with a trimmer European fit, Gildan is boxier and sometimes cheaper at wholesale. Stanley/Stella is clearly better — organic cotton, premium hand-feel — but costs two to three times as much. For everyday basics and printed garments, Fruit of the Loom gives the best value of the three.

Where is Fruit of the Loom made?

Fruit of the Loom is an American brand headquartered in Bowling Green, Kentucky, and owned by Berkshire Hathaway. The brand also runs its own factory in Morocco, where spinning, knitting, dyeing, cutting and sewing happen under one roof. Marmalade Co. buys directly from that factory, which is why its prices are low.
